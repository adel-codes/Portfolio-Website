The infusions

Which infusion, and how much of it, is the doctor's call once you've been assessed. If you can't tell which of these applies to you, that's what the consultation is for.

  • Hydration30 to 45 min

    Saline with electrolytes and magnesium, to support fluid and electrolyte balance if the doctor thinks it suits you.

  • Vitamin C45 to 60 min

    Only after an individual medical assessment, at a dose the doctor sets. It isn't suitable for everyone.

  • Myers cocktail45 to 60 min

    Vitamin C, B-complex vitamins, magnesium and zinc, with the doses set from your assessment.

  • Iron infusion1 to 4 hours

    For iron deficiency or iron deficiency anaemia that pathology has confirmed. Your vital signs are watched throughout, and you may be given a small test dose first.

  • Vitamin D injection15 to 20 min

    An injection into muscle for vitamin D deficiency that a blood test has confirmed, dosed from your results.

  • NAD+ infusion2 to 4 hours

    Given slowly. The medical team adjusts the rate as the session goes.

  • Antioxidant45 to 60 min

    Alpha-lipoic acid, vitamin C, glutathione and selenium together, given under medical supervision.

IV infusions are health services delivered under the supervision of registered medical practitioners. They are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease, and results vary between individuals.

Before an iron infusion

Iron is only given when pathology shows a deficiency, so you'll need results from the last 3 months that include:

  • Serum ferritin
  • Transferrin saturation
  • Full blood count
  • C-reactive protein (CRP)

You'll also need a GP referral. No recent results? The clinic can give you a pathology referral before your consultation.

An iron infusion isn't suitable if you have iron overload or haemochromatosis, or a known sensitivity to iron preparations. About 6 to 8 weeks afterwards, a follow-up blood test shows how you've responded.

How it works

  1. Questionnaire

    Your history, medicines, allergies and goals, online. The $75 fee covers the doctor's review and your consultation.

  2. Assessment

    A doctor reads your answers, then talks with you about whether an infusion makes sense, and which one.

  3. Pathology, where needed

    Iron and vitamin D need recent results. Others may too, depending on your history.

  4. The infusion

    In the clinic, seated, with a cannula in your arm. Trained clinical staff give it, under a doctor's supervision.

  5. Review

    Follow-up and repeat pathology, where the infusion calls for them.

On the day

Drink plenty of water in the hours before and eat something light. Wear sleeves that push up easily. Bring a list of your medicines, supplements and allergies, and any recent blood results.

Plan to stay in the clinic for 10 to 15 minutes afterwards before you drive.

Safety and cost

Most people tolerate a supervised infusion well. Like any drip, it can leave a bruise or some soreness where the cannula went in, and some people feel flushed or slightly sick during it. Allergic reactions are rare. The full medical assessment comes first, and the clinical team is trained to manage a reaction if one happens.

Medicare doesn't cover most IV infusions. Some private health funds pay a partial rebate under extras cover, so ask yours. The clinic gives you a receipt.

Questions people ask first

General information only. Your doctor will answer for your situation.

Do I need a referral?

Not for most infusions, though every one needs a consultation with one of the clinic's doctors first. Iron needs a GP referral and recent pathology. A vitamin D injection needs recent pathology.

How long does a session take?

Hydration and vitamin infusions usually take 30 to 60 minutes. Iron takes 1 to 4 hours, depending on the dose, and NAD+ 2 to 4 hours.

How often can I have an infusion?

That depends on the infusion and on your health. Some people have one session; others follow a schedule. The doctor suggests how often after your assessment.

I have a chronic condition. Can I still have IV therapy?

Ask. The doctor looks at it case by case: some conditions need extra clearance, and some rule out particular infusions. Put your full history, medicines and allergies in the questionnaire so nothing is missed.

What's the difference from oral supplements?

Nutrients you swallow go through the gut, and how much gets absorbed varies. An infusion goes straight into the bloodstream. Whether that difference matters for you is a question for your doctor.
