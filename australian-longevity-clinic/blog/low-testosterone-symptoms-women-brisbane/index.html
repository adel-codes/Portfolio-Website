Low Testosterone in Women: Symptoms and Testing in Brisbane

Testosterone levels in women start falling from the mid-twenties, and the symptoms of low testosterone overlap with other conditions. Here is how it tends to show up, and how a doctor confirms it before anything is prescribed.

Women make testosterone throughout their lives, in the ovaries and the adrenal glands. It contributes to libido and sexual function, and it is thought to play a part in energy and mood, muscle tone and body composition, bone density, and memory and concentration.

When levels fall below the optimal range, the effects can be wide-ranging and can significantly affect quality of life.

When levels fall, and why

Testosterone begins to decline gradually from the mid-twenties. By the forties, levels may be less than half of their peak.

Some things push levels down faster than age alone. Removing the ovaries (surgical menopause) causes an immediate, dramatic drop in testosterone production. The combined oral contraceptive pill works differently: it raises sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), which binds to testosterone and reduces the amount of free, biologically active testosterone available to tissues.

Chronic stress can contribute, because sustained high cortisol levels can suppress adrenal androgen production. Certain autoimmune disorders affecting the adrenal glands or ovaries can impair testosterone production. Hypothyroidism can affect sex hormone metabolism and contribute to low testosterone symptoms.

Symptoms

The symptoms are non-specific and overlap with other conditions. That is why low testosterone can be missed, or put down to stress or depression, or to simply "getting older".

Low libido is among the most commonly reported symptoms. That can mean less interest in sex, or finding it harder to become aroused and to feel satisfied.

Fatigue is common too, the persistent kind that sleep quality and lifestyle don't explain. Rest doesn't fix it.

Mood can change. Low testosterone is associated with higher rates of depression, anxiety, irritability and emotional flatness, and many women describe losing their motivation and drive. Problems with concentration and memory are frequently reported as well, the "brain fog" many women talk about.

Physical changes include reduced muscle tone and more body fat, particularly around the abdomen, and muscle can be hard to build or keep despite regular exercise. Scalp hair can thin and body hair can lessen. Testosterone also plays a role in bone maintenance, so bone density can fall, and a prolonged deficiency contributes to osteoporosis risk.

Many women say they feel flat and less confident, in a way that is hard to pin on any single cause.

How it is diagnosed

Diagnosis needs both blood testing and a clinical assessment. Blood tests alone are not enough: the diagnosis should rest on symptoms and blood results together.

At Australian Longevity Clinic, the blood tests cover:

  • Free testosterone, the biologically active fraction that is not bound to proteins. This is the most clinically relevant measure, because total testosterone can be normal while free testosterone is low.
  • Total testosterone, the overall amount in the bloodstream.
  • Sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG). High SHBG reduces how much free testosterone is available, even when total testosterone appears normal.
  • Oestradiol and progesterone, for the broader hormonal picture and to identify any imbalances.
  • DHEA-S, a marker of adrenal androgen production.
  • A full metabolic panel, to identify contributing factors such as thyroid dysfunction or insulin resistance, and any nutritional deficiencies.

The doctor also looks at your history and the medicines you take; the combined pill, for example, raises SHBG. Testosterone is a Schedule 4 prescription medicine, and nothing is prescribed from the online health questionnaire alone. Sometimes, after all of that, the answer is no.

If treatment is needed

Treatment is considered only when low testosterone is confirmed and clinically significant.

The form most commonly prescribed for women in Australia is a testosterone cream or gel, and it is the approach endorsed by the Australasian Menopause Society for testosterone therapy in women. You apply a small amount to the skin each day, typically on the inner arm or thigh, which gives a steady, low-dose supply. Low-dose subcutaneous injections are used in some women instead, particularly those who prefer a less frequent schedule.

Whatever the form, the clinic always prescribes the lowest effective dose. The goal is to restore levels to the mid-normal physiological range for a woman's age, not to supraphysiological levels. Regular monitoring is essential.

Resistance training, adequate sleep, stress management and nutritional support can all contribute to maintaining healthy testosterone levels, and should be addressed alongside any hormone treatment.

Is it safe?

At appropriate physiological doses, and monitored correctly, testosterone therapy is considered safe for most women. The most commonly reported side effects at low doses are mild acne and increased facial or body hair. They are generally dose-dependent and resolve with dose adjustment.

It is considered appropriate for selected patients, after a thorough medical assessment, with a registered medical practitioner prescribing and monitoring it. It is not appropriate for women who are pregnant or breastfeeding, or who have certain hormone-sensitive conditions.
