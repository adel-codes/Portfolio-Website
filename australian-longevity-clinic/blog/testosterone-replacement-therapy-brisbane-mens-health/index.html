Testosterone Replacement Therapy for Men in Brisbane: Who It Suits and What It Involves

Fatigue and low libido can come from low testosterone or from something else entirely, so the process starts with blood tests and a clinical assessment. This is what TRT involves if they confirm the diagnosis.

Testosterone is the main male sex hormone. It is produced predominantly in the testes, under the control of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis. It is best known for its effects on libido and sexual function. It also influences muscle mass, bone density, red blood cell production, mood, cognitive function, cardiovascular health and metabolic rate.

Levels peak in early adulthood and decline gradually from approximately age 30, at roughly 1–2% per year. That decline is a normal part of male ageing. For some men, though, levels fall below the threshold the body needs to function well, a condition known as hypogonadism or, more colloquially, "low testosterone".

Symptoms

Many of the symptoms are non-specific and easy to blame on stress or poor sleep, or on getting older.

Persistent fatigue and low energy despite adequate sleep are common, as are reduced libido and sexual dysfunction. Muscle mass can fall while body fat increases, particularly around the abdomen. Mood changes include irritability and low motivation, as well as depression. Difficulty concentrating and cognitive fog are common too.

Bone density can fall, which increases fracture risk. Body and facial hair may decrease, and the testes can become smaller.

Because these symptoms overlap with many other conditions, an accurate diagnosis requires blood tests to measure total and free testosterone, alongside a thorough clinical assessment.

How it is diagnosed

Diagnosis begins with a full consultation and blood panel. At Australian Longevity Clinic, that panel covers:

  • Total testosterone, the overall amount in the bloodstream
  • Free testosterone, the biologically active fraction not bound to proteins
  • Sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), which affects how much testosterone is available to tissues
  • LH and FSH, the pituitary hormones that regulate testosterone production
  • Oestradiol, to assess the balance between testosterone and oestrogen
  • Full blood count and metabolic panel, to assess overall health and identify contraindications

A diagnosis of hypogonadism requires both biochemical confirmation (low testosterone on blood testing) and clinical symptoms. Treatment is not recommended based on blood results alone.

Testosterone is a Schedule 4 prescription medicine. No prescription is issued until the doctor has your blood results and medical history and has examined you, and sometimes the answer at the end of that is no.

The first step is an online health questionnaire, which one of the doctors at the clinic (628 Ipswich Road, Annerley, Brisbane) reviews before contacting you within 1–2 business days.

The forms of TRT

Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) aims to restore testosterone to the mid-normal physiological range, relieving symptoms and supporting long-term health. It is available in several forms.

Injections are the form most commonly prescribed at Australian Longevity Clinic. They are typically given weekly or fortnightly, into a muscle or under the skin, and provide reliable, predictable testosterone levels. You can be trained to give them yourself at home.

Testosterone cream or gel is applied to the skin daily, usually on the shoulders or inner arms. It gives stable daily levels, but care is needed to avoid transfer to partners or children.

Pellets are implanted under the skin every 3–6 months for a slow, steady release. They are less commonly used in Australia.

Benefits and risks

In men with confirmed hypogonadism, the potential benefits are more energy and less fatigue, improved libido and sexual function, more muscle and less body fat, and better bone density. Mood and motivation may improve. The evidence for improved cognitive clarity is limited, and a cardiovascular benefit has not been established.

The potential risks and side effects:

  • Raised haematocrit (thickening of the blood), managed with dose adjustment or therapeutic venesection
  • Testicular atrophy and reduced sperm production, which matters if you are considering having children in future
  • Acne and oily skin
  • Fluid retention
  • Possible prostate effects, which is why regular PSA (prostate-specific antigen) monitoring is required
  • Mood changes, particularly with supraphysiological dosing

TRT is not appropriate for men with active prostate cancer, untreated sleep apnoea or certain cardiovascular conditions. A thorough medical assessment is essential before starting treatment.

What being on TRT involves

Starting TRT is a significant medical decision that requires ongoing monitoring and commitment.

The dose is set from your baseline levels and symptoms, and to what you want from treatment. The aim is the lowest effective dose that resolves symptoms and achieves mid-normal testosterone levels.

Blood tests are repeated 6–12 weeks after starting treatment, then every 3–6 months once stable, checking testosterone levels, haematocrit (red blood cell concentration), PSA and oestradiol. Some men need additional medications to manage side effects such as raised haematocrit or oestrogen conversion, and the doctor will discuss these with you as part of your treatment plan.

TRT is typically a long-term treatment. Stopping it abruptly can result in a temporary period of low testosterone while the body's own production recovers.
