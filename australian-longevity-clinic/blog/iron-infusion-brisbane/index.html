Iron Infusion in Brisbane: Who Needs One and What to Expect

Who an iron infusion is for and what happens on the day. It's only prescribed once blood tests confirm a deficiency.

Iron tablets and liquid supplements have to be absorbed through the gut, and their digestive side effects make them hard for many people to tolerate. An infusion bypasses the gut entirely. The iron goes directly into the bloodstream through an intravenous (IV) cannula, so bioavailability is near-complete and those side effects are avoided.

We only prescribe an infusion once a blood test has confirmed iron deficiency, and every infusion is given under the direct supervision of our registered medical practitioners.

Who needs one?

Iron deficiency is one of the most common nutritional deficiencies in Australia. It particularly affects women of reproductive age, including during pregnancy, and people with certain medical conditions.

An infusion may be recommended when oral iron hasn't worked or can't be tolerated. Many people get significant gastrointestinal side effects from oral iron supplements, including nausea, constipation, stomach cramps and dark stools. For them, IV iron is an effective alternative.

It may also be recommended when the deficiency is severe. If ferritin is very low or haemoglobin is significantly reduced, oral iron may not replenish stores quickly enough. IV iron can restore iron levels within days, rather than the months required with oral supplementation.

Poor absorption is another reason. Coeliac disease and inflammatory bowel disease can significantly reduce how much iron is absorbed from the gut, and so can gastric bypass surgery. In those cases IV delivery is the most reliable option.

And some people may need their iron levels restored quickly: athletes and women preparing for pregnancy, for example, or patients recovering from surgery. In that situation, IV infusion can be the better approach.

A diagnosis of iron deficiency anaemia requires both low ferritin and low haemoglobin. Iron deficiency without anaemia (low ferritin, normal haemoglobin) is also clinically significant, and may cause symptoms including fatigue, hair loss, poor concentration and reduced exercise tolerance.

For an infusion at the clinic, your pathology needs to be from the last 3 months and include serum ferritin, transferrin saturation, a full blood count and C-reactive protein (CRP). You'll also need a GP referral.

If you have symptoms such as persistent fatigue, hair loss, shortness of breath or poor concentration, talk to a doctor. Testing comes before any decision about an infusion.

At the clinic, the first step is our online health questionnaire. If you already have recent blood test results, have them ready; they let us assess your suitability at the first appointment.

On the day

Infusions are given at 628 Ipswich Road, Annerley. The infusion itself typically takes between 15 and 60 minutes, depending on the product used and the dose prescribed. With a consultation beforehand and a brief observation period afterwards, the whole appointment is usually 60 to 90 minutes.

The consultation is short. A doctor goes over your blood results and medical history to make sure there are no contraindications. You don't need to fast.

A small cannula is then placed in a vein in your arm, and the iron solution is delivered slowly. You can read or use your phone while it runs, and clinical staff monitor you throughout. Some people notice a temporary metallic taste during the infusion, which resolves quickly.

Afterwards we ask everyone to stay at the clinic for at least 15 minutes as a precaution. Most people feel well straight away and can return to normal activities, including driving themselves home. Mild fatigue or a slight headache in the hours after treatment is occasionally reported, but it's generally short-lived.

How quickly will I feel better?

The timeline for iron stores to normalise varies between individuals. It depends on how severe the deficiency was and on other clinical factors. We recommend a follow-up blood test 6 to 8 weeks after the infusion, to confirm your iron levels have been adequately restored and to see whether any further management is needed.

Side effects and risks

Iron infusions are generally safe when given in a clinical setting by qualified medical professionals, and most people tolerate them well. These are the side effects and risks to know about:

  • Injection site reactions. Mild bruising, redness or discomfort at the cannula site, which typically resolves within a day or two.
  • Skin staining. If iron solution leaks outside the vein (extravasation), it can cause a brownish discolouration of the skin at the injection site. This is uncommon with modern IV iron products and careful technique, but it can be permanent. Our clinical team takes every precaution to minimise the risk.
  • Hypersensitivity reactions. Serious allergic reactions to IV iron are rare but possible. This is why every infusion here is given in a clinical setting with appropriate emergency protocols in place, and why we observe everyone for at least 15 minutes afterwards.
  • Temporary symptoms. Some people have mild flushing, headache, nausea or muscle aches during or shortly after the infusion. These are generally transient and resolve without treatment.

What it costs

The cost of an iron infusion in Brisbane varies between clinics and with the iron product used. Whether a Medicare rebate applies also makes a difference. You'll get a clear cost breakdown at your consultation.

In some cases, iron infusions may attract a partial Medicare rebate. We can advise you on this based on your circumstances.
