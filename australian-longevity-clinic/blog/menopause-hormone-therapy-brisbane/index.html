Menopause and Hormone Therapy in Brisbane: What Changed After 2002

Hormone therapy fell out of favour after the Women's Health Initiative study was published in 2002, and its findings have since been substantially reinterpreted. Here are the options used now, and who they suit.

Menopause is defined as the point at which a woman has not had a menstrual period for 12 consecutive months, marking the end of ovarian reproductive function. In Australia, the average age of natural menopause is 51 to 52 years.

The transition leading up to it, perimenopause, can begin 4 to 10 years earlier. It is often when symptoms are most disruptive. Through these years the ovaries progressively make less oestrogen and progesterone, and less testosterone too. The shift affects many systems in the body, and symptoms vary considerably from one woman to the next.

Symptoms

Hot flushes and night sweats (vasomotor symptoms) are the best recognised, affecting approximately 75% of women. They range from mild and occasional to severe and disruptive, and can significantly affect sleep and daily functioning.

Sleep disturbance is extremely common through perimenopause and menopause, often compounded by night sweats. It can be harder to fall asleep and to stay asleep, and sleep may stop feeling restorative.

Mood and thinking can change. Anxiety, depression, irritability, poor concentration and memory difficulties are frequently reported, and many women say the fogginess or emotional instability feels unfamiliar and distressing.

Genitourinary symptoms are common and often undertreated: vaginal dryness, reduced lubrication, discomfort during intercourse, and increased urinary frequency or urgency. Many women also notice reduced libido during and after menopause, with arousal more difficult and sexual satisfaction changing. Joint aches and muscle stiffness are frequently reported, along with reduced muscle mass, and they contribute to reduced physical capacity.

Two changes play out over the longer term. The hormonal shifts of menopause are associated with increased abdominal fat and changes in cholesterol profile, and with increased cardiovascular risk. Oestrogen is also central to maintaining bone density, so the accelerated bone loss in the years around menopause significantly increases the long-term risk of osteoporosis and fracture.

What happened in 2002

Hormone therapy (HT), previously called hormone replacement therapy or HRT, has had a complicated history. The Women's Health Initiative (WHI) study, published in 2002, caused widespread alarm about the risks of HRT. Prescribing fell dramatically. Many women were left without effective treatment for debilitating symptoms.

The WHI findings have since been substantially reinterpreted. The study used older hormone preparations (conjugated equine oestrogen and medroxyprogesterone acetate) in older women, average age 63, who were well past the menopausal transition. The risks seen in that population cannot simply be applied to younger, recently menopausal women using modern preparations.

For healthy women under 60 who are within 10 years of menopause onset, the current consensus is that the benefits of hormone therapy outweigh the risks. That is the position of leading menopause societies, including the Australasian Menopause Society and the British and North American Menopause Societies.

What is used now

Today's hormone therapy has evolved significantly from the preparations used in the WHI study.

Oestrogen can be given as body-identical oestradiol: patches, gels or sprays that are chemically identical to the oestrogen produced by the human ovary. These differ from the conjugated equine oestrogens used in the older studies, and they appear to have a more favourable safety profile. Because it goes through the skin, this oestrogen does not pass through the liver before entering the circulation. That avoids the increased clotting risk associated with oral oestrogen preparations.

Micronised progesterone (Prometrium, Utrogestan) is body-identical too, structurally identical to naturally produced progesterone. It may have a more favourable cardiovascular and breast safety profile than older synthetic progestogens, although the evidence is limited.

Testosterone may also be considered for some women, particularly those with low libido; the evidence that it helps fatigue or cognitive changes is limited. Our article on low testosterone in women covers it in more detail.

How the clinic approaches it

Nothing is prescribed from the online health questionnaire alone. A doctor reads your history and medicines alongside your pathology before recommending anything. Sometimes the answer is no.

The assessment starts with a full hormonal panel: oestradiol, progesterone, testosterone, FSH, LH, SHBG and thyroid function. A metabolic assessment (fasting glucose, insulin, lipid profile and inflammatory markers) looks at cardiovascular and metabolic risk. For women at increased osteoporosis risk, we can arrange DEXA scanning and provide targeted bone health support.

Any hormone therapy is then matched to your symptoms, health history, risk factors and preferences. We discuss all the options, including non-hormonal approaches, and support you in making an informed decision. Nutrition, resistance training, sleep and stress management are addressed alongside any hormonal treatment.

For women seeking support beyond hormones, peptide therapies are sometimes used alongside hormone therapy as part of a longevity program. They include BPC-157 for gut health and GHK-Cu for skin and tissue repair, along with Epitalon, which is used for cellular longevity and sleep. The evidence for these peptides is limited.

Who it suits

Hormone therapy is appropriate for most healthy women with menopausal symptoms who are under 60 and within 10 years of their last menstrual period. It is most often considered for women with:

  • moderate to severe vasomotor symptoms (hot flushes, night sweats)
  • significant sleep disturbance, or changes in mood or cognition
  • genitourinary symptoms affecting quality of life
  • premature or surgical menopause
  • increased osteoporosis risk

A thorough medical assessment is essential. Hormone therapy needs careful consideration in women with a personal history of oestrogen-sensitive breast cancer, unexplained vaginal bleeding, active liver disease or certain clotting disorders.
